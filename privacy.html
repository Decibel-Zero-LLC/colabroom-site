Privacy Policy

Last updated October 6, 2026

CoLabRoom is published by Decibel Zero LLC ("we", "us"). This describes what the app actually collects and where it actually goes — not a generic template. If anything here stops matching what the app does, tell us at support@colabroom.com.

What we collect

Account

Your email address, a display name, and an optional profile picture.

The emails about your account — confirming your address, resetting a password, changing your email, and telling you that your password or email was changed — come from hello@colabroom.com. Since September 29, 2026 they have been sent by Resend, an email service, so Resend receives your email address and what each email says. Resend sends through Amazon's email service. We turned Resend's open and click tracking off, so these emails carry no tracking image and no tracked links.

What you write and record

Song lyrics and chord annotations, comments, room and song names, and any voice notes or reference recordings you attach to a song. All of it is stored so the people in your room can see and hear it — that's the point of the app.

What you import

Lyric sheets you import (PDF, Word, Excel, and the other formats CoLabRoom reads) are parsed on your own device before anything is saved. A public Google Sheet link is fetched only from the link you supply.

Invitations

The email address of anyone you invite to a room, or anyone you ask to play on a song before they have an account.

What you say about yourself

If you list yourself in the Open Mic: the city you type, what you play, and a few words about the kind of music you make. All three are optional, all three are editable, and all three stop being visible to anyone the moment you take yourself off the list.

If you choose how you read notes — letter names, Do Re Mi, or German names, and the language a sheet's musical words are said in — that choice is kept against your account, and nobody else can see it.

Who you have worked with

When somebody asks you to play on a song, when you answer either way, and when a take you recorded lands on somebody else's song, we record that it happened — who, when, and which part. It exists for one purpose: making the people the app suggests to you better than a list of strangers. It is shown to nobody, there is no score anywhere in it, and it is deleted with your account.

What you say to people

Messages you send to one person, lines you write in a room's thread, replies on an ask, the note you can leave with “heard it”, and the questions you ask the app about a song (except the ones it answers by itself) are all stored, so the people they are for can read them. Each is yours to take back from inside the app. Who can see each one is under “Who can see what” below.

Looking for people

A note that you would like to meet somebody who plays a particular part is stored for a month and shown to musicians who play it. Sending or answering a connection request is stored so the app knows who your people are.

Whether you are here right now

While the app is open it tells our realtime service that you are, so your people — connections, and everybody you share a room with — can see a mark beside your name. That mark is not written down: closing the app ends it, and there is no record of when you had the app open. A door you open and a check-in at a place are different: they are written down, as the next two parts say.

Your door

You can open your door to say you would not mind company while you work, on a song or on nothing. We store when you opened it, when it closes, the song it is open on, who it is shown to, and the last time your phone said it was still there. It goes dark by itself when your phone has not said so for ten minutes, or two hours after you opened it or last kept it open. Your people can see it. Everybody on the Open Mic who may meet you can see it too, but only if you choose that; under 18, a door is shown only to the people you share a room with.

Somebody who can see your door can knock. We store who knocked, when, and whether you let them in. A knock reaches you as a notification, and its card fades after thirty minutes. A closed door is deleted with its knocks about a day after it closes.

Letting somebody in makes a small room for the two of you: a room made at a door. The person you let in can hear the song your door is open on while the door is open. We record who opened the door and who was let in, and keep that for as long as the room exists, even after either account is deleted. The room goes by itself when the door's time is over, unless it holds a song, a set or a picture, or its owner keeps it. The person let in can ask to keep it; the ask is stored for as long as the room exists, and cannot be taken back. Only the person whose door it was can keep the room. Keeping it makes everybody in it each other's people: you can message each other and see when the other is here. The others in the room are told with a card in their inbox; they are not asked first.

Here tonight

Somebody can make a place for one night — a name they type, such as a venue, and when the night starts and ends, at most eight hours — and put up its poster. Checking in with the poster's code records that you are there, when you arrived and when you left. Nothing asks your phone where it is. While the night is on, the people checked in there who may meet you — adults, or people you already play with — see your face, your first name and what you play, and nobody else does. If you open your door there, only the people checked in see it, and they can ask to meet you at a spot inside the place. We keep the spot, the time, who was asked and their answer; a no is never shown to the person who asked. For seven days after the night, the people who were there can still see who was there, unless you said you had left. Then the place is deleted with everything in it. If you type a place code that opens nothing, we note that for an hour, so that codes cannot be guessed.

Open Mic Night

The owner of a room can put one of its songs that is already on the Open Mic on an Open Mic Night list. The list shows who put each song there, and when, to anybody signed in who may hear that song, and is deleted eight weeks after the night. You can take a song you put there off the list. If you ask to be reminded of the night, we keep which of four broad time-zone bands your phone is in, and nothing finer, until you turn the reminder off.

Calls

You can call the people in a room. A call runs through LiveKit, a service that carries the sound and the picture between the phones and browsers in the call while it is on. Calls are never recorded: we never ask LiveKit to record one, and the permission the app is given to join a call does not include recording. When a call has a song up, you can choose Record me to record your own part on your own phone; that is a take on the song like any other. LiveKit is told who is in a call by an account identifier and the display name you chose, so the people in it can see who is talking.

While you are in a call your app tells us every twenty seconds that you are still there, so the room can show who is in it; when you leave, that note is deleted. If your phone could not say goodbye, the room stops showing you within a minute, and the note is deleted within ten minutes. When the first person starts a call, the others in the room who can join it are told, no more than once every ten minutes. We keep a record of when each call in a room started and ended, for as long as the room exists; for a room made at a door, a call's record can stay for thirty days after the room goes, as below.

After a call, the room keeps a note of it: who was on the call, how long it ran, which songs went up, the takes each person recorded and whose words changed, and a line anybody who was there and is still in the room may add, up to 280 characters. To put people in the order they arrived, we keep when each person was first and last heard on the call; nothing adds those times up. Only the people who were on the call can read its note, and only while they are still in the room. A take shows in it only if it has been shared, or is yours. A line from somebody you have blocked, or who has blocked you, is hidden from you. If you leave the room, your place on the call and your line stay in its note for the people still in it. You can take your own line back. Whoever reviews reports can also remove a line somebody reported, as “Reports and blocks” says.

A room made at a door is different, because it goes by itself when the door's time is over. When it goes, the note of a call in it is kept for thirty days, counted from the moment the room goes, if it has at least one line and at least two people from the call are still in the room. It is kept for those people only. Anybody from the call who had already left the room, or been taken out of it, is taken off the note first, with their line, and nobody is told. Any other note goes with the room. A kept note shows who was there, when the call ran and for how long, and the lines; it holds no songs or takes. Somebody on it is shown by name only if you could see their page anyway, and otherwise as “Somebody”. The app shows a kept note under Your music, After the call, with the last day it is there (“Here until 4 November”). There you can take your own line back, for good: it goes from the note for everybody, and cannot be brought back. Nobody can add a line to a kept note. After thirty days it is deleted, by a job that runs every five minutes. If the owner keeps a room made at a door, or it holds a song, a set or a picture, the room stays, and its notes are ordinary notes. The After the call page is new: the versions of the app people have today do not show it yet, so until a newer version reaches you, a kept note cannot be read or taken back from the app.

Because LiveKit charges by the minute, we count the time spent in calls, ten minutes at a time, so that each account's calls can have a monthly limit. It is counted against your account, or, while somebody in the room who pays for a plan is on the call, it can be counted against theirs instead, with a note that it was yours. The count holds nothing about what was said or shown.

Calls, and asking the app about a song, are for people 18 and over for now. Before your first call, unless you have already said, you are asked the month and year you were born, once; the newest version of the app also asks before your first question about a song. We keep that month and year against your account, and nobody else can see it; the app only ever uses it to answer whether you are 18 or over, which calls, asking the app about a song, lesson links, meeting people you have not worked with, who can see your door or see you at a place on Here tonight, and whether a room made at a door can be kept all depend on. If the answer is under 13, the month and year are not kept: only the fact that such an answer was given, and when, so the question is never asked again on that account. Anybody in a call can report or block anybody else in it, from the call itself.

Your code, for meeting people

Every account has a short code, shown as a QR code, so somebody you meet can ask to add you. Anybody signed in who has your code can see your display name, your picture and the parts you play, and can send you a request, unless one of you has blocked the other. If the two of you do not already share a room or a song, it opens only when both of you have said you are 18 or over. Making a new code in the app stops the old one working.

Notifications on your phone

If you turn notifications on, your phone hands us a delivery token. We store it against your account and give it to Google's Firebase Cloud Messaging, which is what actually reaches the device. The token identifies an installation rather than a person: signing into a different account on the same phone moves it, uninstalling makes it stop working and we drop it the next time we try, and deleting your account deletes it. The notification itself carries only what you would see in the app.

The app also tells us when a notification reached your phone — with the app open, with it closed, or because you tapped it — so we can see for ourselves that delivery works rather than guess. That is stored on the notification itself and goes when it goes.

Reports and blocks

If you report something, we store what you reported, which kind of thing it was, anything you typed, and who put it there — and a person reads it. A report of a line in a call's note keeps that line's exact words. Whoever reviews reports can remove a reported line from a call's note: it goes from the note for everybody who was on the call, and the person who reported it is told it was taken down. The person who wrote it is not told. We keep a record of which call the line was in, who wrote it and when, and the removal is written in a log of what reviewers did, which cannot be changed or deleted.

A report is kept with no end date. It stays, with any words it quotes, after the account of the person who made it, or of the person it is about, is deleted; so does the record of a removed line. A recording or picture that an open report names is kept until the report is decided, even if it is deleted in the app.

If you block somebody, we store that you did, so the app can keep the two of you apart. If the two of you share a room made at a door that nobody kept, blocking ends it between you at once. If it holds a song, a set or a picture, it stays with whoever opened the door; otherwise it is deleted, and its call notes are kept for thirty days by the same rule as when a door closes. A block deletes nothing from a note already kept: the person you blocked stays on it as “Somebody”, and your lines and theirs are hidden from each other.

Feedback and diagnostics

If you send feedback, we store its category, your message, the screen you were on, and your app version and platform, against your account, and a screenshot if you attach one. If you send it from a problem the app showed you, what the app said is added under your message. If you are not signed in, the app offers to start an email to support@colabroom.com in your own mail app instead, with the app's version and platform in it. If the app crashes or hits an unexpected error, the same version/platform information and an error message are recorded automatically so we can find and fix it — this doesn't include your song content. Those records are made against your account when you are signed in; when you delete your account they stay, but no longer say whose they were. If blocking somebody has to be tried a second time, that is recorded the same way: it says that your account blocked somebody, and when, but not who. Each time the app is opened it records that a session started, with the version and platform, so an error count has something to be divided by.

Analysis: where a recording actually goes

Analysing a recording is something you ask for, per song — it isn't automatic. When you do:

SupabaseStores the recording, your account data, and every song in the app. Our database and file storage provider.
RunPodRuns our own instrument-separation code on their GPUs, to split the recording into stems.
Google Cloud RunRuns our own chord-detection code, to work out what's being played.
RunPodAlso transcribes the words for a song sheet. That runs on the same worker as the separation, on our own code — the vocal does not leave that machine.
OpenAIFor recordings, used on the shorter path only: voice notes, and recordings you transcribe from Takes. The isolated vocal — not the full mix — goes to OpenAI's transcription API. Governed by OpenAI's own API data terms.
RunPodAlso works out the tune of the isolated vocal. That is where the sung range shown on a song sheet — and on your Open Mic profile, if you have listed yourself — comes from. Same worker, our own code.
Anthropic or OpenAIOnly when you ask the app a question about a song. Your question and what the app has already worked out — the title, key, chords, sections, up to sixty lines of the words, which parts have been recorded on it and the display names of the people who recorded them, and the way you name notes — go to a language model that writes the answer. The recording never does. Which model depends on how we have configured it: Anthropic's API when a key for it is set, otherwise OpenAI's. Both publish API terms under which what is sent is not used to train their models. We keep your question and the answer too; if the model does not answer, the question is kept with no answer. Some questions the app answers by itself: those go to no model, and are not kept. Asking is for people 18 and over.
Apple or GoogleIf you dictate lyrics rather than type them, the phone's own speech recogniser is used, and on iOS that may send what you say to Apple. It's the system feature, not ours, and it only runs while you're dictating.

RunPod and Google Cloud Run are infrastructure we run our own code on — they don't process your recording for their own purposes. Making a song sheet no longer sends anything to OpenAI: transcription moved onto the analysis worker, where it is both more accurate and ours. OpenAI still receives the isolated vocal on the shorter path — a voice note, or a Studio recording you ask for words from — and never the full mix.

One thing that is not a recording also goes to OpenAI: every profile picture, gallery picture and room picture you upload is sent to OpenAI's moderation model, which looks for sexual, violent or graphic images. If it flags one, the picture is never shown and a report is filed for a person to look at. This is governed by the same API data terms.

Analyses are cached by the recording's fingerprint: the exact same audio file, analysed again by anyone, reuses the first result instead of being reprocessed. Nobody can reach an analysis without already having the recording it came from.

Who can see what

A room and everything in it — songs, lyrics, comments, recordings — is visible only to that room's members. Nothing you write or record is public by default, and nothing becomes public because you finished it.

A few separate, deliberate acts can make something public, or show you to people outside your rooms, and each one is yours to take and yours to undo:

  • Listing yourself in the Open Mic. Your display name, your picture, and whatever you entered under “what you say about yourself” above become visible to anyone signed in, and you can be found by searching for those things. Off until you turn it on.
  • Putting a song on the Open Mic. That song, and the takes inside it you have already shared with your own room, can be heard by anyone signed in. A take you have not shared stays private even then — the room offers the song, never your unheard draft.
  • Showing a finished song. Marking a song finished is private and shows it to nobody; it only stops the song asking for help. Putting it in the showcase is a second choice, made separately.
  • Opening your door to the Open Mic. Your door, and the name of the song it is open on, can be seen by everybody on the Open Mic who may meet you, while it is open. Closing it ends that.
  • Putting a song on an Open Mic Night list. The list shows that you put it there, and when, to anybody signed in who may hear the song, for eight weeks after the night. Taking it off the list ends that.
  • Checking in at a place on Here tonight. Your face, your first name and what you play are shown to the people checked in there who may meet you, while the night is on and for seven days after it. Saying you have left takes you off the page at once.

One more thing you can hand to one person: asking a particular musician to play on a song lets that person — and nobody else — hear that song while the ask is open. Withdrawing it, their answering no, or blocking them ends it.

Talking

  • A message to one person is seen by the two of you. Either of you can take back your own lines. Blocking closes the thread in both directions at once.
  • A room's thread is seen by everybody in the room — including people who join later, who can read what was said before they arrived. Leaving the room ends your access to it; deleting the room deletes it.
  • A reply on an ask is seen by the people who can see the ask: whoever asked, whoever was asked, and the room the song lives in.
  • A note left with “heard it” reaches the person who put the song up, as a notification.
  • A note that you would like to meet somebody is shown, for a month, to musicians who play that part and have chosen to be findable.
  • Whether you have the app open right now is visible to your people while it is open, and to nobody else. A door you open is seen by your people, and by the Open Mic only if you choose; a check-in at a place, and a door opened there, only by the people checked in there who may meet you.

A notification about any of these carries the sender's name and the first part of what they said, and nothing more.

The chord tool on this website

The chord tool takes an audio file, works out the chords, and sends them back. It does not require an account and does not create one.

The recording is never stored. It is held in memory for as long as the analysis takes, passed to the chord detector, and dropped. There is no upload folder, no database row, and no copy afterwards — so there is nothing to delete, and nothing for us to hand over if anybody ever asked.

One thing is kept, and only to stop the tool being used to run up a bill: a salted hash of the network address the request came from, with a count of how many songs it has sent today. Not the address itself, and not anything about the music. Each day's rows are deleted the following night, so none is kept more than about twenty-eight hours. They can answer exactly one question — whether this requester has had all twenty-five of today's songs — and no other. Before a day's rows are deleted, we add up two numbers for that day — how many different requesters there were, and how many songs they sent — and keep those two numbers with no end date. They say nothing about anybody.

What this website counts

The pages here add one to a few daily totals. Each total is a day, the name of what happened, and a number. This is the whole list, thirteen names:

  • A page was opened. Every page that counts, every time it loads, reloads included. A page sends its own name with each count, but the counter throws the name away, so this is one total for the whole site.
  • Somebody went towards the app. A click on a link into the app, or on an email link asking for an invite.
  • On the chord tool (the chords and what-key pages): a file was chosen; chords came back; chords did not come back; a visitor's songs for the day ran out; the day's whole budget ran out; the chords were copied; a link to the chart was copied; somebody arrived on such a link; they went on to try a song of their own; a card under the result was followed. Opening a link to a chart counts as chords coming back too. When a song is really analysed, our function counts the answer as well, so chords coming back, or not, adds two.
  • On the transpose, capo and numbers pages: the tool was used, once each time the page loads, the first time any of its controls changes; the chart was copied; a card to another tool was followed. A card into the app counts as going towards the app.

None of these totals names a person. There is no cookie, no visitor ID, no pixel, no analytics service, and nothing that says one visit was the same person as another. The counting goes to the same function of ours the chord tool posts to, and these legal pages count nothing at all. The code that counts reads only the name of what happened and a flier code (below), never your network address. The request still reaches Supabase, who run that function for us, the way any request on the web does; we have not yet checked what Supabase's own logs keep of it. We keep the totals with no end date.

The transpose, capo and numbers pages also load their fonts from Google Fonts, so your browser asks Google for them, as it would any web server, and Google sees your network address. Every other page here uses our own copy of the fonts.

One thing is written into your browser, and only if you arrived on a link carrying a code — a QR on a flier, a card at a gig, a link under a video. The code names the flier, not you. Your browser remembers it, with no end date, so that we can tell which flier worked. The visit that arrives with the code is counted against that flier, and so are chords coming back and every click towards the app for as long as your browser remembers it. The transpose, capo and numbers pages pass it on to the chord tool, where it counts as an arrival again. A link into the app carries it too, and the app keeps its own copy for seven days. If you make an account while the app still has it, or sign in to an account less than a week old, the account keeps the code until you delete the account, and one sign-up is counted against the flier; that count stays after the account is gone. The app does the same with its own links: an account can remember that it came from an invitation, a lesson link or a Here tonight poster. Arrive without a code and there is none to remember. Clearing this site's data in your browser removes this site's copy. It does not remove the app's copy, which belongs to app.colabroom.com, or the code on an account already made.

How long we keep it

Most of it, until you delete it or delete your account. Some things go sooner, on a clock, and a few stay after an account is deleted; both are listed below. There's currently no automatic expiry on feedback or diagnostic records — we're telling you that plainly rather than implying a cleanup schedule that doesn't exist yet.

A take is never deleted because nobody has played it for a while. It stays until somebody removes it: whoever recorded it, the room's owner, or whoever deletes the song or the room it is on.

Messages and threads stay until whoever wrote a line takes it back, the room is deleted, or the account is. A note looking for somebody expires after a month. Questions you asked the app, and its answers, stay with your account. The note that you are in a call is deleted when you leave it, or within ten minutes if your phone could not say so. The month and year you were born, if you gave them, stay with your account until you delete it. The record of when a room's calls started and ended, and the note of each call, go when the room does — except a note kept after a room made at a door went, which is deleted thirty days after the room went; you leave every call's note, kept ones included, when you delete your account. The count of time spent in calls stays after an account is deleted, with nothing left in it that says whose it was. A delivery receipt lives and dies with the notification it belongs to.

On a clock: a knock's card in your inbox fades after thirty minutes; a place code that opened nothing is forgotten after an hour; a closed door is deleted with its knocks about a day after it closes; a place on Here tonight is deleted, with who checked in and any meets, seven days after its night; a kept call note, thirty days after its room went; an Open Mic Night list, eight weeks after its night. A reminder for Open Mic Night stays until you turn it off. How you read notes stays until you change it or delete your account. When you say Not now to some of the app's offers — listing you on the Open Mic, its welcome questions, its first-run card — we remember that against your account, and on your phone, so it does not ask again: for thirty days for the Open Mic, and for good for the other two. That stays until you delete your account.

A few things stay after an account is deleted. A report is kept with no end date, with any words it quotes, and so is the record of a line a reviewer removed and the log of what reviewers did. A room made at a door keeps the account identifiers of who opened the door and who was let in for as long as the room exists. Diagnostic records stay without saying whose they were. The totals this website counts, and a sign-up counted against a flier, have no end date; none of them names a person.

Your controls

  • Delete a voice note, a recording, or a song at any time from inside the app.
  • Export a song or setlist as text or a printable PDF.
  • Take back any message, reply or note you wrote, from the line itself. A line in a call note that was kept after its room went is taken back under Your music → After the call, until the note's last day, and that is for good.
  • Choose some of the notifications you get under Account → Notifications: invites, answers to them, being asked to play, messages, calls and new takes each have a switch, and the Open Mic Night reminder comes only if you ask for it. Other notifications have no switch yet, among them a knock on your door, being let in, an ask to meet and its yes, a room you are in being kept, and the answer to a report you made.
  • Make a new code for meeting people, which stops the old one working.
  • Leave a room you no longer want to be in — its songs and its thread go with it. Your place on its calls and your lines in their notes stay for the people still in it; in a room made at a door, they are deleted from its notes when the room goes.
  • Close your door, say you have left a place on Here tonight, or take your song off an Open Mic Night list, at any time.
  • Delete your account from Account → Delete account. This is immediate, permanent, and has no recovery window — see the account-deletion details on the CoLabRoom home page for exactly what it removes, including what happens to rooms you own. It also deletes every message you sent, in rooms and to people, your questions to the app, your notes and your connection requests. It does not delete words of yours quoted in a report, or the other things “How long we keep it” lists as staying after an account is deleted.
  • Can't sign in? Email support@colabroom.com from the address you signed up with.

Children

CoLabRoom isn't directed at children under 13, and we don't knowingly collect information from anyone under 13. Calls are for people 18 and over; when somebody gives a birth month and year that makes them under 13, the app refuses it and does not store the month or year, only that an answer like that was given, and when, so it cannot be answered again. If you believe a child has created an account, contact support@colabroom.com and we'll remove it.

The Open Mic is for adults. Under 18, the rooms you are invited into work as they always do. Under 18, you can open your door, but it is shown only to the people you share a room with; and you can check in at a place on Here tonight, but there you see, and are seen by, only people you already play with.

Changes

If this policy changes in a way that matters — a new third party, in the analysis chain or anywhere else, a change to what we keep — we'll update the date at the top. We mean to say so inside the app too, for anything material, but the app has no place to say it yet; until it does, the date above is the sign that something here changed.
